Patterns
========

* Initially, the prototype has to be loved by the people that are using
  it

* Sales pitch for version 1.0 is to small customers, and it goes
  something like "this will make your least favorite part of your job go
  away"

* This is my favorite part because it involves making people's lives
  better

* making a product people want to use only matters when people have
  autonomy and purchasing power

* Once you start selling to big customers, employee buy-in matters less

* Big customer sales pitch is more like "replace your high cost
  employees with our technology and low-cost, lower-skilled workers."

* VC investors want "hockey stick" growth

  "In the derogatory sense, a business started with the intention of
  becoming a startup, but which instead becomes a smaller business is
  often referred to as a lifestyle business by investors or other
  detractors."

  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lifestyle_business

* For b2b businesses, you need big customers

* Big customers mostly care about profits (boost revenue or cut costs)

* Products go from being useful to becoming spyware

OnShift
=======

* Prototype in 2006: find somebody to work a shift by blasting texts to
  qualified and available employees

* $250k angel investment in 2007 meant my partner and I could quit day
  jobs

* By 2009, the software help managers plan / maintain / update / revise
  schedules

* Most customers were skilled nursing facililities (SNFs) or hospitals.

* Hours per resident day (HPRD) is a metric that says roughly "if you
  have this many patients in your facility as of midnight, you have to
  have at least this many hours scheduled."

* Think like teacher:student ratios in class rooms, but nurse:patient
  instead

* Ohio has a 2.5 HPRD, so each patient is promised 2.5 hours of nursing
  care that day.

* One nurse working five hours can take care of two patients.

* 100 patients in beds as of midnight means you need 250 scheduled hours
  for that day.  If your nurses work 12-hour shifts, you need 21 nurses.

* The system, among other things, found people to cut when scheduled
  hours exceeded HPRD regulations.

* We claimed that the product would pay for itself by identifying
  "extra" scheduled labor beyond legal minimums

* We added features meant to reduce labor costs:

    * Reports showing people that clock in early or clock out late

    * Discourage giving shifts to high-wage employees when lower-cost
      employees were available

    * Require approval for overtime scheduling

Relevance to y'all
------------------

A product that improves the quality of life of workers is not easy to
sell if workers aren't really involved in the decision making process.

There is a deep chasm separating health care providers (doctors, nurses,
nurse assistants, etc) and health care facility owners.  

The human experience of medical care doesn't show up in the financial
reports.

Complion
========

* To run a drug or medical device trial, you have to keep a lot of
  paperwork, like:

    * tracking patient consent, perhaps with regular renewals

    * track patients joining and leaving 

    * protocols used in the study may change and changes need to be
      documented

    * verify credentials of clinicians and facilities

* As of the 2010s, many of these drug trials used literal 3-ring binders
  in hallway file cabinets to track studies.

* To build it, we interviewed as many clinical trial coordinators as we
  could 

* We built a "one size fits most" product with built-in checklists and
  guides 

* Our product became "the way to run drug trials" at a lot of hospitals

* PE acquired Complion, combined competing products into one product

Relevancy to ILR
----------------

* Clinical Coordinators could now run vastly more trials. Does that mean
  more wages or layoffs?

* We created a single workflow and since it was so much better in some
  ways, people adopted it.

* There are people run thinking that the way our system works is now the
  official way to do it.

* But we designed this system based on what we could get done quick, so
  we could sell it.

* Founder of Complion sent me this text recently:

    "...a software industrial complex has changed how companies are run

    structures, particularly due to enterprise software)"

* In other words, new technology can codify silos into existence

Decision Desk
=============

* Applying to musical conservatories, art schools, and theater programs
  involves submitting samples of work (e.g., concert footage).

* Professors review the work and maybe admit them.

* DecisionDesk was a software platform to replace a very messy and
  error-prone process

* Pretty quick, the founders signed up a small number of very happy
  customers.

* After VC investment, DecisionDesk started work on a new product and an
  aggressive sales campaign.

* Larger universities signed up and revenue spiked!

* Every new contract came with so many new feature requests that it
  killed margins

* The app now worked differently for different customers.  Customers
  needed more help using it.  Again, this also killed margins.

* Customer churn increased, usually due to bugs in new features

* We couldn't raise more money and costs were way above revenue, so it
  shut down

Relevancy
---------

* Processing applications for music school was a complex, crooked
  process

* Sometimes there is no obvious "one size fits all solution"

* Workflow automation is not easy when people have fundamentally
  different methods

Streamliner
============

This is a product I built for a client to use in-house.  My client is a
liquidator. 

He has a bunch of warehouses in central Ohio.  He keeps a team of people
that unpack truck loads of returned and discontinued or out of demand
goods from retailers like Target, CVS, Walgreens, Amazon, other places.

The unpackers followed guidelines like: 

Expensive and lightweight items should be listed on online and shipped.

A different set of goods should be sold in their own discount store
(food, apparel, big toys, etc).
n

Some items should be packed into different shipping containers.

I made a system where the workers used bluetooth ear pieces and cheap
android tablets.  They would search for any bar code they could, scan
it, and the system would recommend a container to put the item into.

I got there a few weeks later and five people were now doing the work
that used to be like twenty people.

The boss makes more money now.  But this is Massillon, Ohio.  There's no
other jobs nearby for the people.

Eventually the client stopped buying returned goods from Amazon because
Amazon improved their own systems and the mix of items in containers
changed away from stuff like wristwatches (very lucrative) and towards
stuff like apparel.

I saw first hand how producers of apparel and books  create about 2x to
5x of the expected sales and plan to throw it out.

This stuff takes weeks or months to get to market and the production
systems are scheduled out, so the worst thing is for there to be
customers looking for a product and having to wait.  Customers will
pick a substitute.

I saw a pile of copies of Sarah Palin's autobiography big enough to fill
up my house.  

The incremental cost of printing extra is almost zero.
